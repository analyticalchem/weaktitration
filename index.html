Student guide

This page does the equilibrium arithmetic so you can concentrate on the chemistry. Work through the activities in order; each Load button sets the page up for that activity. Before you move the titration point, predict what will happen to the pH and to the bars, then check.

What you are looking at

  • The curve is pH (y-axis, 0–14) against the volume of titrant that has been added (x-axis, mL). The titrand starts in the beaker; the titrant comes from the burette.
  • The dot is the current point in the titration. Drag it, use the slider, or type a volume.
  • The bars show how many moles of each form of the titrand are present at that point (starting form first, most-titrated form last), and — once you press Show H₃O⁺ / OH⁻ — below a dividing line, the moles of free OH⁻ and H₃O⁺ actually present in the beaker at that moment (the titrant's own ion is listed first). Their product is fixed by Kw, so as one grows the other shrinks. The titrant's ion is not the amount you have poured in: before the equivalence point nearly every OH⁻ added has reacted with the titrand, so the bar stays tiny; past equivalence it grows as unreacted titrant piles up. Switch the bar axis to Log to watch that tiny amount change by powers of ten — on a log axis, equal steps are equal factors, so a bar twice as long is not twice as much.
  • Dashed guides mark each equivalence volume (top), each half-equivalence volume (bottom), and the pH that equals the pKa (left).

Activity 1 · The four regions of a weak-acid titration

25.0 mL of 0.100 M acetic acid (pKa 4.76) titrated with 0.100 M NaOH. Drag the dot slowly from 0 mL to 50 mL and stop at these places:

  1. 0 mL — only the weak acid. pH ≈ 2.9, not 1.0. Look at the bars: almost all HA, a tiny sliver of A⁻. Why isn't it pH 1 like 0.100 M HCl would be?
  2. 0 → 25 mL — the buffer region. Each OH⁻ converts one HA into one A⁻ (watch the two bars trade) and is used up doing it — the OH⁻ bar stays at essentially zero even though you keep adding base. The pH creeps upward slowly. At 12.5 mL (half-equivalence) the two bars are equal and pH = pKa. That is the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation made visible.
  3. 25.0 mL — the equivalence point. The moles of OH⁻ delivered equal the moles of HA you started with. HA is gone; the beaker holds sodium acetate. pH is 8.73, not 7 — the acetate ion is a weak base, and the small OH⁻ bar you see (about 3 × 10⁻⁷ mol) is exactly what its hydrolysis produced.
  4. Past 25 mL — excess strong base. Now the OH⁻ bar takes off: nothing is left to react with it, so every drop of titrant stays as free hydroxide. The pH is set by that leftover OH⁻ alone.

Check yourself: at 20.0 mL, what is the HA : A⁻ mole ratio? Use it to predict the pH with Henderson–Hasselbalch, then compare with the dot.

Activity 2 · What pKa controls

Leave the dot at half-equivalence and change only the pKa: try 3.0, 5.0, 7.0, 9.0.

  • The whole buffer region slides up and down with pKa, but the equivalence volume does not move. Why not?
  • The starting pH rises and the equivalence pH rises too. Weaker acid → stronger conjugate base → more basic equivalence point.
  • The vertical jump gets shorter as pKa increases. Around pKa 9–10 it is barely a jump at all: this is why very weak acids are hard to titrate with an indicator.

Activity 3 · Dilution

Same acid, but 0.00100 M titrated with 0.00100 M NaOH. Same equivalence volume, same pKa, yet the curve is squashed: the start is less acidic, the equivalence point less basic, and the jump is small. Compare the bars — the shapes are the same but the axis is a thousand times smaller. The ratio HA : A⁻ sets the pH in the buffer region, not the absolute amounts.

Activity 4 · A weak base titrated with a strong acid

25.0 mL of 0.100 M ammonia (pKb 4.75) titrated with 0.100 M HCl. The curve is the mirror image of Activity 1: it starts basic and falls.

  • Each H₃O⁺ from the acid converts one NH₃ into one NH₄⁺ — the bars trade places just as HA and A⁻ did, and the H₃O⁺ bar stays near zero until the base runs out.
  • At half-equivalence the pH equals the pKa of the conjugate acid NH₄⁺, which is 14.00 − pKb = 9.25. The dashed guide is labelled that way.
  • At equivalence the beaker holds NH₄Cl and the pH is 5.27 — below 7, because NH₄⁺ is a weak acid.

Activity 5 · A triprotic acid: phosphoric acid

H₃PO₄ has three protons, so there are three equivalence volumes (25, 50 and 75 mL) and the bars now show four species. Drag from 0 to 100 mL.

  • Between each pair of equivalence points there is a buffer region, and at each half-way point the pH ≈ the matching pKa.
  • Notice the third jump is missing. pKa3 = 12.35 is so high that HPO₄²⁻ is barely neutralised before the solution is already very basic — the third proton can't be titrated in water.
  • At the first equivalence point (25 mL) the beaker is essentially all H₂PO₄⁻, an amphiprotic species. The textbook estimate is pH ≈ ½(pKa1 + pKa2) = 4.68; the exact answer here is 4.70. Why is the shortcut slightly off? (Hint: it assumes the H₂PO₄⁻ concentration is much larger than Ka1. Here it is 0.050 M against Ka1 = 7 × 10⁻³ — not "much larger".)

Activity 6 · Sodium carbonate titrated with HCl

CO₃²⁻ is a diprotic base: CO₃²⁻ → HCO₃⁻ → H₂CO₃. This is the classic two-end-point titration (phenolphthalein at the first, methyl orange at the second). Find both equivalence points and identify which species dominates at each. Why is the second jump sharper than the first?

Activity 7 · When pKa values are too close

Citric acid's three pKa values are only about 1.6 units apart. The three buffer regions merge into one long slope and the intermediate equivalence points disappear, so citric acid titrates like a single triprotic acid with one sharp end point at 75 mL. Look at the bars at 37.5 mL: three species coexist. The rule of thumb is that you need pKa values ≳ 3 units apart to see separate steps. Notice too that the dashed "pH = pKa" guides no longer meet the curve — the half-equivalence shortcut only works when the steps are well separated.

Questions to take away

  1. Why is the pH at the equivalence point of a weak acid–strong base titration above 7, but below 7 for a weak base–strong acid titration?
  2. You have an unknown monoprotic weak acid. From its titration curve alone, how would you find (a) its pKa and (b) the number of moles you started with?
  3. Two acids have pKa 4.0 and 8.0. Which gives the sharper end point with a strong base, and why?
  4. In Activity 5, by 50 mL you have poured in 5.0 × 10⁻³ mol of OH⁻, yet the OH⁻ bar shows only a few micromoles. Where did the rest of the hydroxide go? Use the species bars to account for it.

How the numbers are computed

No approximations are used, and no region-by-region formulas. For an acid HnA with acidity constants Ka1 … Kan, the fraction of the titrand that has lost j protons is

αj = [H⁺]n−j·Ka1···Kaj / D,    D = Σk=0..n [H⁺]n−k·Ka1···Kak

and the solution must be electrically neutral. With everything diluted into the total volume, that single condition is

[Na⁺] + [H⁺] + CA·Σj (z₀ − j)·αj − Kw/[H⁺] − [Cl⁻] = 0

where z₀ is the charge of the fully protonated form (0 for HnA, +n for a base like NH₃ or CO₃²⁻ once it has taken up all n protons), [Na⁺] is what the strong-base titrant delivers and [Cl⁻] what the strong-acid titrant delivers. The left-hand side increases steadily with [H⁺], so the page finds the one [H⁺] that makes it zero by repeatedly halving an interval (a bisection search), then reports pH = −log[H⁺] and the αj at that point. A weak base is simply the same system started from the fully deprotonated end; pKb values you enter are converted with pKa + pKb = 14.00. Activities are treated as ideal (activity coefficients = 1) at 25 °C.
