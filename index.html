Student Guide

This page does the equilibrium arithmetic so you can concentrate on the chemistry. Work through the activities in order; each Load button (and the Activity chips at the top) sets the page up for that activity. Before you move the current point, predict what will happen to the pH and to the bars, then check.

What you are looking at

  • The curve is pH (0 to 14) against the volume of titrant added, in mL. The titrand starts in the flask; the titrant comes from the burette.
  • The current point is where you are in the titration. Drag it along the curve, move the slider, or type a volume.
  • The bars show how many moles of each form of the titrand are in the flask at that point, starting form first. Tick Show free H₃O⁺ and OH⁻ to add the water ions below a dividing line: these are the amounts actually in solution, not what you have poured in. Before the equivalence point nearly every OH⁻ added has reacted with the titrand, so its bar stays tiny; past equivalence it grows as unreacted titrant piles up. Switch the bar axis to Log to watch that tiny amount change by powers of ten (on a log axis equal steps are equal factors, so a bar twice as long is not twice as much).
  • The dashed guides mark each equivalence volume, the dotted guides each half-equivalence volume, and the dash-dot guides the pH that equals the pKa.

Activity 1 · The four regions of a weak-acid titration

25.0 mL of 0.100 M acetic acid (pKa 4.76) titrated with 0.100 M NaOH. Drag the point slowly from 0 mL to 50 mL and stop at these places:

  1. 0 mL, only the weak acid. pH ≈ 2.9, not 1.0. Look at the bars: almost all HA, a sliver of A⁻. Why isn't it pH 1 like 0.100 M HCl would be?
  2. 0 to 25 mL, the buffer region. Each OH⁻ converts one HA into one A⁻ (watch the two bars trade) and is used up doing it, so the OH⁻ bar stays at essentially zero even though you keep adding base. The pH creeps upward slowly. At 12.5 mL (half-equivalence) the two bars are equal and pH = pKa: the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation made visible.
  3. 25.0 mL, the equivalence point. The moles of OH⁻ delivered equal the moles of HA you started with. HA is gone; the flask holds sodium acetate. pH is 8.73, not 7, because the acetate ion is a weak base, and the small OH⁻ bar you see (about 3 × 10⁻⁷ mol) is exactly what its hydrolysis produced.
  4. Past 25 mL, excess strong base. Now the OH⁻ bar takes off: nothing is left to react with it, so every drop of titrant stays as free hydroxide. The pH is set by that leftover OH⁻ alone.

Check yourself: at 20.0 mL, what is the HA : A⁻ mole ratio? Use it to predict the pH with Henderson–Hasselbalch, then compare with the readout.

Activity 2 · What pKa controls

Leave the point at half-equivalence and change only the pKa: try 3.0, 5.0, 7.0 and 9.0.

  • The whole buffer region slides up and down with pKa, but the equivalence volume does not move. Why not?
  • The starting pH rises and the equivalence pH rises too. A weaker acid has a stronger conjugate base, so its equivalence point is more basic.
  • The vertical jump gets shorter as pKa increases. Around pKa 9 to 10 it is barely a jump at all, which is why very weak acids are hard to titrate with an indicator.

Activity 3 · Dilution

Same acid, but 0.00100 M titrated with 0.00100 M NaOH. Same equivalence volume, same pKa, yet the curve is squashed: the start is less acidic, the equivalence point less basic, and the jump is small. Compare the bars: the shapes are the same but the axis is a thousand times smaller. The ratio HA : A⁻ sets the pH in the buffer region, not the absolute amounts.

Activity 4 · A weak base titrated with a strong acid

25.0 mL of 0.100 M ammonia (pKb 4.75) titrated with 0.100 M HCl. The curve is the mirror image of Activity 1: it starts basic and falls.

  • Each H₃O⁺ from the acid converts one NH₃ into one NH₄⁺; the bars trade places just as HA and A⁻ did, and the H₃O⁺ bar stays near zero until the base runs out.
  • At half-equivalence the pH equals the pKa of the conjugate acid NH₄⁺, which is 14.00 − pKb = 9.25. The guide is labelled that way.
  • At equivalence the flask holds NH₄Cl and the pH is 5.27, below 7, because NH₄⁺ is a weak acid.

Activity 5 · A triprotic acid: phosphoric acid

H₃PO₄ has three protons, so there are three equivalence volumes (25, 50 and 75 mL) and the bars show four species. Drag from 0 to 100 mL.

  • Between each pair of equivalence points there is a buffer region, and at each half-way point the pH is close to the matching pKa.
  • The third jump is missing. pKa3 = 12.35 is so high that HPO₄²⁻ is barely neutralised before the solution is already very basic; the third proton cannot be titrated in water.
  • At the first equivalence point (25 mL) the flask is essentially all H₂PO₄⁻, an amphiprotic species. The textbook estimate is pH ≈ ½(pKa1 + pKa2) = 4.68; the exact answer here is 4.70. Why is the shortcut slightly off? (It assumes the H₂PO₄⁻ concentration is much larger than Ka1; here it is 0.050 M against Ka1 = 7 × 10⁻³, not "much larger".)

Activity 6 · Sodium carbonate titrated with HCl

CO₃²⁻ is a diprotic base: CO₃²⁻ → HCO₃⁻ → H₂CO₃. This is the classic two-end-point titration (phenolphthalein at the first, methyl orange at the second). Find both equivalence points and identify which species dominates at each. Why is the second jump sharper than the first?

Activity 7 · When pKa values are too close

Citric acid's three pKa values are only about 1.6 units apart. The three buffer regions merge into one long slope and the intermediate equivalence points disappear, so citric acid titrates like a single triprotic acid with one sharp end point at 75 mL. Look at the bars at 37.5 mL: three species coexist. The rule of thumb is that pKa values need to be about 3 units apart to give separate steps. Notice too that the dash-dot "pH = pKa" guides no longer meet the curve: the half-equivalence shortcut only works when the steps are well separated.

Questions to take away

  1. Why is the pH at the equivalence point above 7 for a weak acid titrated with a strong base, but below 7 for a weak base titrated with a strong acid?
  2. You have an unknown monoprotic weak acid. From its titration curve alone, how would you find (a) its pKa and (b) the number of moles you started with?
  3. Two acids have pKa 4.0 and 8.0. Which gives the sharper end point with a strong base, and why?
  4. In Activity 5, by 50 mL you have poured in 5.0 × 10⁻³ mol of OH⁻, yet the OH⁻ bar shows only a few micromoles. Where did the rest of the hydroxide go? Use the species bars to account for it.

About the Model

How the pH is calculated

No approximations and no region-by-region formulas. For an acid HnA with acidity constants Ka1 … Kan, the fraction of the titrand that has lost j protons is

αj = [H⁺]n−j · Ka1 ··· Kaj / D,   D = Σk = 0 … n [H⁺]n−k · Ka1 ··· Kak

and the solution must be electrically neutral. With everything diluted into the total volume, that single condition is

[Na⁺] + [H⁺] + CA · Σj (z₀ − j) · αj − Kw / [H⁺] − [Cl⁻] = 0

where z₀ is the charge of the fully protonated form (0 for HnA, +n for a base such as NH₃ or CO₃²⁻ once it has taken up all n protons), [Na⁺] is what the strong-base titrant delivers and [Cl⁻] what the strong-acid titrant delivers. The left-hand side increases steadily with [H⁺], so the page finds the one [H⁺] that makes it zero by repeatedly halving an interval (a bisection search in log [H⁺]), then reports pH = −log [H⁺] and the αj at that point.

Weak bases

A weak base is the same system started from the fully deprotonated end. The pKb values you enter (first protonation first) are converted with pKa + pKb = 14.00, so ammonia (pKb 4.75) runs as the ammonium ion system with pKa 9.25, and the carbonate ion (pKb 3.67, 7.65) as carbonic acid with pKa 6.35 and 10.33.

What the bars show

Each form of the titrand is its fraction αj times the starting moles. The free H₃O⁺ and OH⁻ are [H₃O⁺] × V and [OH⁻] × V from the same solution, with [H₃O⁺][OH⁻] = Kw, so as one grows the other shrinks. The species table also lists the total titrant added and how much of it lies beyond the final equivalence point.

Assumptions

Ideal solutions at 25 °C: activity coefficients of 1 and Kw = 1.0 × 10−14. Example pK values are literature values at 25 °C and zero ionic strength. Volumes add. Concentrations below about 10⁻⁴ M are allowed but the curves there are dominated by water's own ions.
